# The Ghost Train

### A Mystery in Five Parts

---

## Part One: Smoke and Mirrors

The call came through at fourteen minutes past ten on a Saturday night, and Detective Sergeant Ray Maguire knew — the way coppers sometimes just *know* — that it would ruin the rest of his life.

"Fire at Luna Park. The Ghost Train. People trapped inside."

He was three beers deep at the Commodore Hotel in North Sydney, watching a middleweight bout on the mounted television and thinking about nothing in particular. He put his glass down, left a tenner on the bar, and drove to Milsons Point with the window cracked against the winter air.

He could see the glow before he reached the harbour. An ugly, pulsing orange, like a furnace door left open against the black water. By the time he parked and pushed through the gathering crowd, the Ghost Train building was fully involved — timber and painted plywood and decades of accumulated grease feeding a fire so hot that the firefighters couldn't get within ten metres of the entrance. A sign above the door still read *Hell's Doorway*, and now it meant what it said.

Maguire stood with his hands in his coat pockets and watched the structure fold in on itself in a shower of sparks. Somewhere behind the police tape, a woman was screaming. Not the theatrical kind you hear in films — a raw, animal sound, the kind that comes from a mother who has just understood something that will never be undone.

Her name was Jenny Godson. Her husband John and their two boys, Damien and Craig, were inside. She'd left the queue to buy ice creams. She'd been gone three minutes.

By dawn, seven bodies had been pulled from the wreckage. John Godson and his two sons. Four schoolboys from a Catholic college in the eastern suburbs — Richard, Michael, Jonathan, and Seamus — all twelve and thirteen years old, out on a rare Saturday night without their parents. They'd been playing rugby that afternoon. They'd been alive.

Maguire wrote their names in his notebook and underlined them. He would carry that notebook for twenty years.

---

## Part Two: The Official Story

The investigation, such as it was, lasted a few weeks. Maguire wasn't lead — that honour went to a senior detective named Grainger, a heavy man with a red face and an instinct for the path of least resistance. Grainger's theory crystallised fast: faulty wiring, or maybe a discarded cigarette. Riders were known to smoke on the train despite the signs. The tunnel was pitch black, lined with painted timber and papier-mâché monsters. A single spark would have done it.

The coroner agreed. In September, he delivered his findings: probable electrical fault or careless smoker, cause ultimately undetermined. Luna Park's management had ignored fire brigade warnings to install sprinklers. They'd ignored council directives about emergency lighting and exit signage. But the negligence, the coroner ruled, was not criminal. Not quite.

Case closed. Move on.

Maguire couldn't.

He'd been at the scene early enough to catch details that never made it into Grainger's report. He'd spoken to a ride operator named Tommy Parsons, a pimpled nineteen-year-old who was working the River Caves next door when the fire broke out. Tommy said he'd smelled kerosene ten minutes before the first flames appeared. Not smoke. Not burning rubber. Kerosene. The sharp, unmistakable bite of it. Maguire wrote it down.

He'd also spoken to a couple — tourists from Melbourne — who'd been standing near the Ghost Train entrance around ten o'clock. They described three men in leather jackets loitering near the back of the building. One of them, the woman said, had been carrying something. A container of some kind. When the fire started, the three men didn't run toward the screams like everyone else. They walked the other direction, calmly, toward the car park.

Maguire wrote that down too.

When he brought these statements to Grainger, the senior detective barely looked up from his Racing Guide. "Ray, the coroner's made his finding. Electrical fault. You want to chase bikie phantoms, do it on your own time."

So he did.

## Part Three: The King of the Cross

Every city has a shadow. Sydney's shadow had a name: Victor Kovac.

Maguire had never met the man, but every copper in New South Wales knew the legend. Kovac ran nightclubs, illegal casinos, and massage parlours from his fortress on Darlinghurst Road in Kings Cross. He had judges in his pocket and police commissioners on speed dial. He collected real estate the way other men collected stamps — ruthlessly, strategically, and often through intermediaries who ensured his name never appeared on a single deed.

Three weeks after the fire, Maguire sat in the Mitchell Library and pulled every planning document he could find relating to Luna Park and the Milsons Point foreshore. What he found made the hairs on his neck stand up.

For two years before the fire, a development consortium called Harbour Vista Holdings had been quietly acquiring leasehold interests around the park. Their proposed project — luxury harbourside apartments with opera house views — needed one thing to proceed: Luna Park had to close. The park's lease was ironclad, protected by government guarantee. The only way to break it was if the park became commercially unviable. If, for instance, a catastrophic event caused it to shut down indefinitely.

Maguire dug into the consortium's corporate records. Layer after layer of shell companies, nominee directors, and offshore trusts. But at the bottom of the stack, referenced in a single footnote in a Companies Office filing, he found a familiar name: V.K. Enterprises Pty Ltd.

Victor Kovac.

Maguire sat back in his chair and stared at the ceiling. Seven people dead — six of them children — so a gangster could build apartments.

He took his findings to Superintendent Driscoll, who listened with his arms folded and his jaw tight. When Maguire finished, Driscoll said five words: "Leave Kovac alone, Ray."

"Sir—"

"That's not a suggestion."

---

## Part Four: The Long Game

Maguire didn't leave it alone. He just learned to be quieter about it.

Over the next decade, he built his case the way a mason builds a wall — one brick at a time, in the dark, with no one watching. He tracked down the Melbourne tourists and took formal statutory declarations. He found Tommy Parsons, now working on a cattle station near Dubbo, and got him to repeat his kerosene story on tape. He cultivated a source inside Kovac's operation — a bookkeeper named Delvecchio who handled the legitimate side of things and nursed a growing conscience.

Delvecchio was the one who gave Maguire the name: Carl Jessup. A career arsonist. A man who had made a quiet living lighting fires for insurance claims and property developers for fifteen years without ever being charged. According to Delvecchio, Jessup had been contracted by an associate of Kovac's to "create a problem" at Luna Park on the night of June ninth. Nobody was supposed to die. The Ghost Train was supposed to be empty — the fire was meant to start after closing. But the park stayed open late that night, and Jessup, already inside with his kerosene and his rags, didn't check.

"He lit it and walked out the back," Delvecchio told Maguire in a parked car on a rain-slicked street in Surry Hills. "By the time he heard the screaming, it was too late."

"Where's Jessup now?"

"Dead. Car accident near Lithgow in '83. Single vehicle. Went off the edge of a mountain road at three in the morning."

"Convenient."

"Everything around Kovac is convenient, Detective."

Maguire asked Delvecchio if he'd testify. The bookkeeper went pale and said nothing for a long time. Then he opened the car door and disappeared into the rain.

Two weeks later, Delvecchio's flat in Darlinghurst burned to the ground. The fire was ruled accidental. Delvecchio himself vanished. His body was never found.

---

## Part Five: The Names

Years passed. Kovac grew old but never soft. He built his empire higher and wider, untouchable behind his wall of lawyers and compromised officials. Luna Park closed, opened, closed again — its fate tied to forces the public never saw. Maguire made Inspector, then Chief Inspector, and all the while he kept his notebook in the inside pocket of his jacket, the seven names still underlined.

It was 1997 when Maguire finally caught his break.

A young constable from Internal Affairs named Sarah Tan came to him with a box of documents she'd pulled from a storage facility in Parramatta — files from an old police corruption investigation that had been quietly buried in the mid-eighties. Among them was a handwritten memo from a senior officer, now deceased, confirming that two detectives on the original Luna Park investigation had received cash payments from an intermediary linked to V.K. Enterprises. The memo named Grainger as one of the recipients.

Grainger, who had steered the investigation toward electrical faults. Grainger, who had dismissed every witness statement that pointed to arson. Grainger, who had retired in 1988 to a waterfront property in Manly that was well beyond a detective's salary.

Maguire and Tan took the documents to the newly formed Police Integrity Commission. It took another two years of hearings, cross-examinations, and legal manoeuvring, but in the end, the wall cracked. Grainger, confronted with bank records and the dead senior officer's memo, broke down in a closed hearing and admitted he'd been paid to bury the arson evidence. He named the intermediary. The intermediary, facing his own charges, named Kovac.

Kovac was never convicted. He died in 2001, in a private hospital room overlooking the harbour, surrounded by lawyers to the last. But the findings of the Commission were made public. The word *arson* appeared in an official document for the first time. The cover-up was acknowledged. The conspiracy, if not proven beyond doubt in a court of law, was laid bare for anyone willing to look.

On the anniversary of the fire, Maguire drove to Milsons Point and stood where the Ghost Train had been. The site was a concrete slab now, waiting for something new to be built. The harbour glittered in the winter sun. A small bronze plaque on a nearby wall listed seven names.

He took out his notebook — the same one, battered and soft with age — and opened it to the first page. The names were still there, still underlined in his careful hand. He read them aloud, one by one, to no one in particular. The wind carried them out over the water.

Then he closed the notebook, put it back in his pocket, and walked away.

He'd done what he could. It hadn't been enough. It never is, in cases like these. But the names were spoken. The truth, or something close to it, was on the record. And sometimes, Maguire thought — sometimes that has to be enough.

---

*Author's Note: This story is a work of fiction inspired by the real tragedy of the Luna Park Ghost Train fire on 9 June 1979, which claimed the lives of John Godson, Damien Godson, Craig Godson, Jonathan Billings, Richard Carroll, Michael Johnson, and Seamus Rahilly. The detective, the gangster, and all named characters other than the victims are fictional. The real case remains officially unsolved. The victims are remembered.*
